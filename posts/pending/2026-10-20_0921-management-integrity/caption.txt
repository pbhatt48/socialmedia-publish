"Does the company have a management of unquestionable integrity?" — Philip A. Fisher, Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (1958)

Fisher placed this question last among his fifteen points, and treated it as the one that could override all the others.
A minority shareholder cannot audit every decision. Compensation, related-party dealings, how bad news is disclosed: all of it rests on the trustworthiness of the people in charge.
Brilliant operators without integrity can make the business thrive while the owners do not. Few analytical edges survive a management that keeps the spoils for itself.
The clues are rarely hidden: candor when results disappoint, accounting that is conservative rather than clever, and incentives that rise and fall with the owners’ own returns.
